How BetaLog reads your climbing

What the grades mean, what the app calls your level, and how the pyramid, the goals and the dots are worked out from what you log.

1

Climbing grades ↑ top

A grade is a shared guess at how hard a climb is. Two systems appear in BetaLog, one per kind of climbing.

V-scale — bouldering

Short, hard problems without a rope. V0 is the easiest, and the scale is open-ended; V17 is the current top of the world. Every step is a real jump: a V4 climber does not send V5 problems just by trying harder for a few weeks.

V0V1 V2V3 V4V5 V6V7 V8V9 V10V11 V12V13V14V15V16V17

French — lead and top rope

Roped climbing on longer routes. Grades run 4, 5, 5+, then a number with a letter and an optional plus: 6a, 6a+, 6b, 6b+, 6c, 6c+, 7a and so on up to 9c. Each letter step is roughly half a V-grade, so a French rung is a smaller move than a V rung.

455+ 6a6a+6b6b+ 6c6c+7a7a+ 7b7b+7c7c+ 8a8a+ 8b8b+8c8c+9a9a+9b9b+9c
Gym grades drift. Two walls can call the same problem V3 and V5. BetaLog only ever compares your climbs with your own climbs at the same gym, so it stays honest as long as you log what the wall says.

Outcomes you can log

FlashSent first go, no falls. SentClimbed clean, top to bottom, on any attempt. ProjectWorked on it, did not send. Still counts as a try. AttemptHad a go, fell. Same as a project for the maths.

Levels ↑ top

A level is a word for a band of grades. BetaLog shows it as a coloured badge on the climbing widget, next to your base grade, and colours every grade you see by its band.

BeginnerV0 – V14 – 5+

The bands are a convention, not a measurement. They are close to what most guides and gyms use, and they exist so a badge can say something short. Your level comes from the grade you own, not the hardest thing you have touched once.

3

Base, Best and Flash ↑ top

Three numbers describe your climbing in each discipline. All three are read over the last 180 days, so they describe the climber you are now, not a season two years ago.

BaseThe hardest grade you have sent at least 8 times in the window. The grade you own. This is what your level badge is read from, what a goal card calls Currently, and what your friends see. BestThe hardest single send in the window, however rarely. One good day is enough. FlashThe hardest grade you have sent first go.

Base is deliberately lower than Best. Sending a 6c once says you can get up a 6c. Sending eight of them says you are a 6c climber. The gap between the two is the honest state of most climbers, and the pyramid below is what fills it.

No base yet means the log does not hold 8 sends at any one grade in the last 180 days. It is not a judgement, only the app declining to guess. Log more and it appears.
4

The grade pyramid ↑ top

The pyramid is the oldest tool in climbing coaching. It says that to be ready for a grade you want a broad base of sends underneath it, in a rough 2 : 1 shape: for each send at the top you want two at the grade below, four below that, and eight below that.

Ready for 6c looks like
6a8 of 8
6a+4 of 4
6b2 of 2
6b+1 of 1
6cthe goal

Widest row at the top, the target at the bottom. The chart on your goal card is this, filled from your log.

Three rules

  • Harder sends flow down. Ten sends at 6b+ fill the 6b+ row and spill into 6b, 6a+ and 6a beneath it. Climbing above a row covers it. A single send at the target fills nothing but its own row, which is the whole point.
  • The target row is not the base. The empty row at the bottom is the normal state of a goal; not having done it yet is why it is a goal. Readiness is scored on the rows above it only.
  • A pyramid is as strong as its thinnest row. Three full rows and one at half count as half ready. One missing row is a missing row, however much sits below it.

What the label says

Base completeEvery row under the target is full. Base nearly thereThe thinnest row is three-quarters full or more. Base formingAround half. Base thinA quarter or so. No base yetA row is empty. Pyramid completeBase complete and the target is sent.

Under the chart the goal card tells you the smallest thing that would help: Log 3 more 6a+s to fill the base. That is always the thinnest row.

5

Goals ↑ top

A grade goal is a target grade, a date, and one of two kinds. They are different goals and the app treats them differently.

Send 6cDone on one send or flash at 6c or harder, in that discipline, inside the 180-day window. The first 6c is the one you remember. Own 6cDone when your base reaches 6c: eight sends at the grade. The slower, bigger goal.
  • Goals tick themselves off. Log the send and the goal moves to Achieved, dated to the send, and a row appears in History that day.
  • A send before the goal was set still counts. If you already sent 6c last month, the app will not let you set Send 6c; it tells you when you did it and offers Own 6c instead.
  • One goal per type. One boulder goal, one rope goal, one of each cardio type, one weight. Edit the one you have rather than stacking a second.
6

The dots ↑ top

Five dots on every goal answer one question: at the rate you are climbing, will you make the date? They come from the forecast below, as a ratio of the time it needs to the time you have.

Time needed, as a share of time leftDots
Half or less: as long again to spare
Up to the deadline
Up to half again past it
Up to two and a half times past it
Further than that, or the date has passed

The words beside the dots are about the base, not the date. Base forming with five green dots means the base is thin but a year is plenty. Base complete with one red dot means it is built but the date has gone. The two are allowed to disagree; that is the point of showing both.

With no deadline, or too little logged to measure a rate, the dots fall back to showing how built the base is. An unanswerable question should not look like a bad answer.
7

The forecast ↑ top

Ready for 6c around March at your current rate. That date is three pieces of arithmetic on your log, added up.

1. Fill the baseSends still missing from the rows under the target, divided by how fast you have been sending those grades since your first climb in the window. 2. Move upHow long a grade change takes you, measured from past changes in your log. With none to measure, a stated default: about four months per V-grade, two per French rung, slower the higher you go. Skipped entirely if you have already sent the target. 3. Own itOnly for an Own goal: the sends still needed on the target's own row, at your rate on that grade, or your base rate if you have never sent it. The card says which.

The card splits the date into those parts so each can be judged on its own, and says where a default was used rather than a measurement.

Climbing weekly: a second line under the forecast runs the same sum at one session a week, two sends a session. It is a lever, not a prediction: at your rate, March; climbing weekly, December. It never changes the dots.

What the forecast is not. It is not a percentage chance of success. Turning a pyramid into a probability would need thousands of climbers with known outcomes, and that data does not exist. A date and a margin can be checked by hand; a percentage here would be invented.
8

Where this comes from ↑ top

The pyramid and its 1 · 2 · 4 · 8 shape come from standard coaching material, going back to Eric Hörst's How to Climb 5.12. The level bands, the 180-day window, the eight-send rule for owning a grade, and the default time per grade are conventions chosen to be fair to an ordinary climber. None of them is a measurement of you, and every one of them is written down in the open so it can be argued with.

Everything the app then says is a statement about your log: what is in it, what is missing, and what that arithmetic projects. It is not advice about what to climb, and a grade with no base under it is not a grade you cannot do, only one the log has nothing to say about yet.
